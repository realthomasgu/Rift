Rift guide

Files, disks and printers

Your files

Your home folder is on the encrypted part of the drive, with the usual folders in it: Documents, Downloads, Pictures, Videos, Music. Screenshots go in Pictures and screen recordings in Videos.

Files, in the dock and in the Applications menu, shows them. Its sidebar has your home folder, those five and the trash. Double-click a file to open it in the app for its kind, or right-click it to open it in another, cut, copy, rename or throw it away. Delete moves the selection to the trash, and Undo at the bottom of the window brings it back; Shift and Delete deletes it for good, after asking. A copy pasted where the name is taken asks first, and keeping both is the answer it offers: choose Replace and the one that was there goes to the trash, so nothing is ever written over.

Files is what a folder opens in, wherever you open it from. An app that offers Show in folder, or asks the system to show a file it has just saved, opens the folder in Files with that file picked out for you.

The trash stands in the dock while there is something in it, and in the sidebar it lists what is in it and where each thing was. Right-click something to put it back, or empty the whole trash. Files other apps move to the trash are there too, and so is whatever you have thrown away on a disk you plugged in: every disk keeps its own trash.

Pictures, and what a file is

The main menu, or Ctrl and G, shows the folder as a grid instead of a list: a tile for each thing, with a small picture of it where there is one. Rift makes those pictures the first time it shows a folder, in the background, and only for the ones on screen, so a folder of a thousand photographs opens at once and fills in as you scroll. They are kept in the usual place under your home folder, which is the same place every other desktop keeps them, and never on the disk the file is on: a stick you plugged in to read is not written to.

Ctrl and I, or Properties at the bottom of the right-click menu, says what Rift knows about what you have selected: its name, its kind, how big it is, where it is, when it last changed, how many pixels across a picture is, and who may read and change it. The size of a folder is worked out while you look at it.

Finding a file

The magnifier in the header bar, or Ctrl and F, turns the path into a field. What you type is looked for in the names of everything in the folder and under it, as you type, and the Folder column says where each one is. Press Enter and the same words are searched by meaning instead: search for bicycle repair and the file about fixing your bike comes up, whether those words are in it or not. That one needs the index of your home folder, which the Search page in Settings makes and keeps up to date; if there is none yet, Files says so and leaves the names it found. Notes, code and PDFs are all in that index, so a letter you were sent is found by what it says in it.

Timeline

Everything in home is snapshotted every hour, so a file you have just ruined is usually still there. The clock in the header bar shows the folder you are in as it was at one of those moments, with the files that have changed since and the ones you have deleted. Earlier and Later step through the moments, and Back to now leaves.

Restore brings the file you select back into the folder as it is now, and with nothing selected Restore everything brings back what the folder held at that moment. A file of the same name that is there now is not written over: Files asks first, and if you choose Replace the one that was there goes to the trash. A name the moment did not hold is left alone, so anything you have made since stays where it is. Replacing a folder replaces the whole folder: the one that is there now goes to the trash as it stands, with everything it has gained since, and the one from the snapshot takes its place. Nothing is lost either way, and you can open the trash and take back whatever you want.

Only your home folder is snapshotted, so a folder on a disk you plugged in, or on the exchange partition, has no clock in its header bar. See Snapshots, backups and a second drive for the snapshots themselves and for backups on another disk.

Other disks

Disks shows every disk in the machine, its partitions and what its own health counters say. You can read all of that.

What you cannot do is write to a disk that belongs to the computer. Mounting, formatting, partitioning and unlocking one are refused, and no disk of the machine is mounted by itself. That is the rule the whole system is built on: the computer you borrowed is the same when you take the drive out.

A memory stick or an external disk you plug in is yours to use. It appears in Files under your folders as soon as it is plugged in. Nothing is mounted until you click it, and the button at the right of its row unmounts it and ejects it, so you can pull it out. A mounted disk also stands in the dock, where a click opens it and a right click ejects it.

A disk that is encrypted appears with the others and says it is locked. Click it and Rift asks for its passphrase, opens it and shows you what is on it, the same as any other disk. Rift does not keep the passphrase.

If your drive was written with an exchange partition, it is at /exchange and Files lists it with the disks. That is the plain place to put a file for another computer to read. It is not encrypted, so keep out of it anything you would not leave on a memory stick. In Ghost mode it is listed as a place that is not mounted, and selecting it mounts it, since nothing of the drive is mounted there until you ask.

Printing

Rift prints to printers that describe themselves over the network, which is every printer sold in the last few years. There are no drivers to install and none can be installed. A printer that only works with software from its maker is not one Rift prints to.

A printer on the network is found by itself and appears in the print dialog of an app. Rift asks the network what is there and announces nothing about the machine it is running on.

Scanning

Document Scanner reads from a scanner over USB or over the network and saves a PDF, a JPEG or a PNG. Network scanners are found the same way printers are.

The camera and the firmware

Camera takes a picture or a video from the camera of the machine. The first time an app asks for the camera, a window asks whether to allow it, and the answer is kept.

The firmware of the machine is read and never written. To see what each device runs:

fwupdmgr get-devices
fwupdmgr security

The second command says whether Secure Boot, the TPM and the IOMMU are in use on this machine. A firmware update from Rift is refused, because the machine it would be written to is whichever one the drive is plugged into.
